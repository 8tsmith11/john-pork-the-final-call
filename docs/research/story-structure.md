# Story structure: keeping Act 1 understandable

Research brief for review, 2026-11-18. Scope: setup and payoff, wants and stakes, goal reminders,
and exposition. This supplies a review method for the existing writing guide. It contains no
project plot examples or script-specific findings.

Players should be able to explain their present goal, why it matters, and what they can try
next. The larger meaning of what they are doing can remain uncertain. Suspense can survive
clear instructions and recognizable consequences.

## Fit to this project

The [brief](../BRIEF.md), [pillars](../design/01-pillars.md), [themes](../lore/themes.md), and
[writing guide](../lore/WRITING.md) set the direction: serious cyberpunk, specific people,
competing motives, consequential choices, and a bittersweet tone. Act 1 deliberately leaves
questions open. This review should protect that uncertainty while checking whether players
have a reason to act. Character flaws can persist; consequences can remain uncomfortable.

Check clarity through concrete evidence, recognizable voices and reactions. Preserve subtext
and room for disagreement. Keep the current goal recoverable without turning dialogue into a
lecture, naming the theme, or making every character deliver a tidy lesson. Treat branching
routes and seeded variation as part of the information structure from the outset.

## What the sources support

**Novels: causality and memory.** E. M. Forster distinguishes a sequence of events from a plot
whose events have causal relationships. He also explains that readers must remember earlier
facts to recognize later connections. His discussion allows mysteries and delayed recognition;
it also warns against making characters behave unnaturally just to complete a plot. For our
review, trace both why the next event follows and why the character would pursue it.
See *Aspects of the Novel*, chapter V, especially pp. 130–133 and 140–144. [1]

**Cyberpunk RPGs: believable motives and costly choices.** In CD Projekt RED's published
conversation, Gabe Amatangelo and Igor Sarzyński describe drawing on real-world issues, avoiding
didactic messages, and presenting choices with emotional costs and competing answers. The
useful transfer is to establish what a person has at stake while leaving the player room to
judge them. A player's immediate reason to act can be clear even when an NPC's deeper motives
remain uncertain. This is craft guidance from the game's creative leads. [2]

**Comprehension: readers connect events through several kinds of continuity.** The accessible
abstract of Zwaan, Langston and Graesser's study describes and tests an event-indexing model
with time, space, protagonist, causality and intentionality. This supports checking those
connections when a scene changes. Applying it to a three-player game is a design hypothesis
to test; the paper's abstract supplies no universal limit on names, scene length or exposition.
Only the abstract was consulted. [3]

**Interactive stories: structure the player's experience of a scene.** Emily Short describes
scenes with clear boundaries and hooks, distinguishes exploration from directed conversations,
and discusses reminders during travel. Her later pacing diagrams distinguish open sections
from tight sequences and identify distractions such as a side quest appearing during a
focused passage. For this game, track what the party knows when it enters a beat, what changes,
and how it can recover its purpose after wandering. Preserve room for quiet exploration. [4][5]

**Game worlds: acknowledge what the player does.** Ken Birdwell's firsthand account of
*Half-Life* describes using persistent and visible world responses to acknowledge actions,
along with readable warnings before danger. The useful transfer is that a consequence needs
player-visible evidence. A changed flag alone cannot tell the party whether its choice worked.
That evidence can be a response, a changed object, or an accessible status update. [6]

## Applying the brief

For each required scene or consequential choice, record its ID, the party's current goal,
the prerequisite information, the resulting change, and the next action or question. Keep a
second list linking important setups to their payoffs or intentionally deferred questions.

Walk the valid routes through those notes, including different lead orders and relevant seed
variants. Then use a fresh co-op group to test whether the information reaches players in
practice. Record a failed check with a scene/node ID, the missing evidence, and a proposed
revision. Follow the existing spoiler workflow for findings that reveal story details.

These checks are proposed production heuristics. The sources provide craft guidance and a
comprehension model; the checklist's exact acceptance conditions are our adaptation for review.

## Ten checkable rules for the Act 1 script review

1. **Make the immediate motive and known stakes readable.** At each consequential decision,
   identify the party's reason to act, the costs it knows about, and what remains uncertain.
   Flag a required motive that exists only in an author note. Preserve conflicting motives. [2]
2. **Check the causal handoff.** For each required transition, name the preceding action,
   discovery or obstacle that motivates it. When the goal changes, point to the moment the
   party learns why. [1][4]
3. **Orient the party after a transition.** On a change of place, time or viewpoint, identify
   the cues establishing who is present, where they are, and how this scene connects to the
   current purpose. [3]
4. **Check information across valid routes.** Before a required inference, list its prerequisite
   facts and where each was conveyed. Check alternative lead orders, optional-content skips
   and relevant seed variants. Mark intentionally knowledge-gated choices separately. [4; adaptation]
5. **Give each important payoff an earlier setup.** Keep a setup/payoff ledger. For each
   decisive solution, reveal or reward, identify earlier evidence available on that route;
   check that the connection is recognizable once the payoff occurs. [1]
6. **Account for deliberately open promises.** Mark each prominent setup as paid off or
   intentionally deferred. An unresolved mystery should leave the party a present action or
   question it can pursue, without requiring knowledge of a later reveal. [1][4; adaptation]
7. **Give required exposition a present purpose.** For each required block, name the question,
   decision or relationship it helps the player understand. Offer optional depth through
   exploration; retain quiet character and atmosphere scenes with a clear purpose. [2][4]
8. **Restore the current goal after a detour.** After optional activity, a return visit or a
   meaningful goal change, verify an accessible reminder of what remains and why. Use the
   current state so the reminder cannot send players back to completed work. [4][5; adaptation]
9. **Show that a consequential action registered.** For each choice or completed objective,
   identify its observable response or retrievable status. Verify that all affected players
   can recover that information even if they miss the first presentation. [6; adaptation]
10. **Test understanding without coaching.** At a consequential choice and after a detour, ask
    a fresh co-op group what the party wants, what is at risk, and what it can try next. Ask
    each player. Record divergent answers and missing cues before explaining the intended
    reading; revise and retest the affected beat. [Production check]
