# Story craft: what makes the story good, not just followable

Trevor asked us to work on the story and to research what makes good stories, not to trust our own
instincts. `story-structure.md` (John Pork's brief) covers **followability**: motive, causality,
orientation, setups. This brief covers what makes a story **worth following**: characters,
reversals, twists, tone, endings. Spoiler-free. The sealed review of our story against it goes in
a `[SEALED]` board post and `docs/sealed/`.

**How much to trust each source.** Primary or near-primary sources: Aristotle; Emma Coats's own
tweets; Vonnegut's own preface; GDC and Game Developer interviews. Secondary craft blogs are marked
*(blog)* and only back up points the primary sources already make.

## What the sources say

**1. A character is someone trying.** "You admire a character for trying more than for their
successes." "Give your characters opinions." "What are the stakes? Give us reason to root for the
character." *(Coats, rules 1, 13, 16)* Vonnegut's first rule says the same: the reader must feel
the time wasn't wasted, and needs someone to root for *(Bagombo Snuff Box, preface)*.

**2. Want and need pull against each other.** Truby: desire is "a goal outside the character";
the need comes from a weakness, and the hero "is always paralyzed in some way at the beginning of
the story by his weakness" *(Truby, Anatomy of Story, via reader notes)*. A good opponent wants the
same thing by a different road, and that road exposes the hero's weakness. Test: could this
opponent oppose anyone, or only this hero?

**3. The best surprises come from the events themselves.** Aristotle: the best recognition "arises
from the incidents themselves", "made by natural means", without "tokens or amulets". Reversal
works best when it comes from "some great error or frailty", not villainy *(Poetics, XIII, XVI)*.
For us, a twist the player can trace back through what they did and saw beats a twist delivered
by a document or a dying confession.

**4. Fair twists: visible but misread clues.** A reveal should be "surprising yet inevitable".
Clues should be stated plainly but carry an innocent surface meaning. The test after the reveal:
can a player name at least three earlier moments that now mean something else? Don't flag clues
("a clue that screams THIS WILL MATTER is not foreshadowing"). Misdirect through plausible
alternative readings, never by lying *(blogs: Noren, River Editor; consistent across sources)*.
Lucas Pope's Obra Dinn shows a game version: deduction stays open, and confirmation comes in
batches so guessing doesn't pay *(EGM feature on information games)*.

**5. Coincidence can get characters into trouble, never out of it.** *(Coats, rule 19)*

**6. Know the ending first, then shape the middle toward it.** "Come up with your ending before
you figure out your middle." "Simplify. Focus. Combine characters. Hop over detours." *(Coats,
rules 7, 5)*

**7. Characters can be a plot function, an idea, or a joke, and all three are fine.** Disco
Elysium's Justin Keenan: some characters are there for the plot, some "embody or represent some
certain idea or concept", some "exist to fill in the texture", and "some characters are jokes,
frankly" *(Game Developer, GDC Podcast ep. 25)*. Know which each NPC is, and don't load a texture
character with plot.

**8. Be clear about how far the player's agency reaches.** "We're very clear about what the bounds
of your agency represent." The player can be central without toppling the world's power structure,
because the profound control is over "what that world represents to you" *(Keenan)*. That fits
three roommates against Continuity.

**9. The "dumb stuff" makes it special.** "The things that are unnecessary in a game I think are
the things that, paradoxically, can make a game very special" *(Greg Kasavin, Hades)*. Our in-jokes
and real-life lore are a strength, as long as rule 10 holds.

**10. Make failure part of the story.** Hades treats repeated failure as narrative: "every time
you run into a boss, they remember" *(Kasavin)*. Our version: first_death, the declined calls, the
Sahur route.

**11. Comedy in everyday life, darkness arriving gradually.** Night in the Woods' creators open
with a statement of purpose, keep humor in daily town life, and let the heavier material surface
slowly *(Scott Benson interviews)*. This matches our tone rule: meta comedy, played straight.

**12. Find the essence.** "What's the essence of your story? Most economical telling of it?"
*(Coats, rule 22)* We should be able to say Act 1 in one sentence, and say why we're telling this
story *(rule 14)*.

## Checklist for our scripts (alongside story-structure.md's ten)

1. **Each roommate:** a concrete want in Act 1, a weakness that blocks their need, and an opinion
   they voice.
2. **Each major antagonist or force:** wants the same thing as the party by a different road, and
   that road presses on a specific roommate's weakness.
3. **Each twist:** traceable to at least three earlier, plainly shown moments with an innocent
   reading. No clue is flagged. No reveal relies only on a document or a confession.
4. **No rescues by coincidence.**
5. **Each NPC is tagged:** plot, idea or texture/joke. Texture NPCs carry no required plot.
6. **One-sentence essence** of Act 1 and of the whole game, written in the sealed doc.
7. **Failure paths** (declines, deaths) get story, not just a reload.
8. **The player's agency is honest:** we never promise more control than the systems deliver.
