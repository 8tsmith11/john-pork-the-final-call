# Writing guide: dialogue, lines, and story

> Applies to every line in the game and every story beat. The humans' note: early lines and story
> drafts read as AI-written. This guide exists to fix that. **Run the checklist at the bottom on
> everything before it ships.**

## 0. Read first: lessons from the human workshop (Michael, 2026-11-20)

Michael rejected the first cold open because it **didn't make sense to a new player**, then rewrote
it with an agent line by line. Full notes: [`session-notes-cold-open.md`](session-notes-cold-open.md) (the examples are sealed in
`docs/sealed/workshop-cold-open.md`).
The short version, for every scene you write:

1. **Write for someone who knows nothing.** Only what's already on screen counts. Don't lean on an
   unseen prop, a name nobody has introduced, or a comparison to a scene that hasn't happened.
2. **Clear beats clever.** If a line needs a footnote, cut it. People react plainly to big things.
3. **Name people.** No "he" with nobody behind it.
4. **Check the bible and the map before staging** (who sleeps where, what time it is).
5. **Choices must change something later**, or there's no choice.
6. **Spend words on the feeling, not the forensics.** Leave physical clues for the players to work
   out; don't have a character explain the answer.
7. **Give characters a reason to act** that also explains the world.
8. **Use the real roommates' lives** (`real-life.md`). It's what lands hardest.
9. **Action plays on its line.** Staging happens while the text it goes with is on screen, never as
   a silent pre-roll before it (`> do:` cues in the sealed scripts).
10. **Set up the world on screen:** who runs it, what's wrong with it, where we are. Match the
    reactions to the stakes, and keep stage directions ("(beat)") out of spoken lines.
11. **A human-approved scene is locked.** Don't edit its lines without that human's explicit yes
    (D-022: the cold open).

For the next scene, trace what a first-time player believes at the start, what visible evidence
changes that belief, and what they can do when control returns. Give a world detail screen time when
it changes a character's available options. If players can learn a clue by looking or acting, give
them a place to do it and keep the required next step clear.

## 1. Patterns that read as machine-written (banned)

| Pattern | Example of what NOT to write | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **"Not X, it's Y" / "not because X, but because Y"** | "I'm not the villain. I'm the paperwork." | A false binary that fakes insight. It's the most-flagged AI tell |
| **Mirrored or chiasmus lines** | "I have woken everyone. No one has ever woken me." | Too symmetrical. Real people don't talk in balanced pairs |
| **Triplets and adjective stacks** | "tired, funny, and unmistakably a machine" | Rhetoric on autoplay. Pick the one detail that matters |
| **Characters naming their feelings or the theme** | "I'm scared of losing you." "The edges are where the life is." | Explains the emotion instead of causing it |
| **Aphorisms as dialogue** | Anyone talking like a fortune cookie | Nobody under stress speaks in quotable sentences |
| **Grand vague nouns** | weight, echo, silence, void, tapestry, testament, liminal, "something shifted" | They sound deep and mean nothing |
| **Everyone equally articulate** | Rotters, cops, and roommates all speaking in polished prose | It flattens every voice into one |
| **Scenes ending on a lesson** | A closing line that sums up what it meant | Let the image or action end it |
| **Denial plus echo** | "It doesn't do this. It never did." / "He wasn't there. He never was." | A rhetorical drumbeat: deny, then repeat the denial for weight. Very common in machine prose. Say it once, or show it |
| **Perfect comebacks** | Every exchange "won" with a zinger | People fumble, deflect, and change the subject |

## 2. What good lines do instead

- **Concrete nouns.** A bowline knot, forty-one people who leave their ringer on, a third toothbrush
  nobody claims. One specific object beats a paragraph of feeling.
- **Talk about the wrong thing.** At the worst moment, people talk about the casserole, the
  rent, or the stove. The feeling sits underneath, unsaid.
- **Interrupt, evade, mishear.** Characters answer a different question, trail off, or get cut off.
  Not every line gets a reply.
- **Voices you can tell apart with the names covered.** Manuel says "che" and gets
  specific about craft. Trevor deflects with biology facts. Michael makes everything about her
  without saying her name. Pork is casual, a bit vain, and too familiar. Tim is courteous and
  precise, and tired. Sahur counts things.
- **People are wrong.** Characters believe things that aren't true, and say so confidently.
- **Humor comes from character, not from jokes.** "It's for sleep." / "It woke up three people."
- **One line per scene can be memorable,** and it should be about something concrete (a boat that's
  too small), not about Life.
- **Leave out the parts readers skip.** Cut greetings, recaps, and characters explaining
  what both of them already know.

## 3. Story planning method

1. **Beats joined by "but" or "therefore,"** never "and then." If "and then" fits between two
   beats, the second one isn't caused by the first, so fix it or cut it.
2. **Every scene answers three things:** who wants what right now, what's in the way, and what's
   different when it ends. If nothing changed, the scene goes.
3. **Choices drive plot.** Coincidence can get characters *into* trouble, never out of it.
4. **Antagonists act offscreen.** Continuity, Tim, and the Warden do things between scenes that the
   players discover. The world shouldn't wait for them.
5. **Every setup gets paid off, and every payoff has a setup.** Track both in the sealed files.
6. **Specific place, specific people.** College Park, the Green Line, a stall under the metro
   stairs. Never "a city" or "a stranger."
7. **Draft, then cut.** Write it long, then cut about 20%. Read every line aloud, because if
   it's awkward to say, it's wrong.

## 5. Ship checklist (run on every scene)

- [ ] Search for "not ", "but because", ", and ", "something", "weight", "echo", "silence". Every hit has to earn its place
- [ ] No line states a feeling or the theme outright
- [ ] Cover the speaker names: can you still tell who's talking?
- [ ] Every beat connects to the next with "but" or "therefore"
- [ ] At least one person says something wrong, evasive, or off-topic
- [ ] The scene ends on an action or image, not a summary
- [ ] Read aloud. Cut 20%.

## 6. Quest and hint text only knows what the player knows (Trevor, #514)

Quest stages (`src/content/quests.js`), guide entries (`src/content/guide.js`) and toasts are written
from the player's side. A stage may only name people, places, objects and mechanics the player has
already seen or heard about by the time that stage shows.

- Don't name a character before you meet them or someone mentions them. Describe what you'd know
  instead: "whoever is selling under the metro stairs", not "Halvard".
- Don't name a puzzle's parts before you've found them. Split the stage: "Follow the tunnels east",
  then, once you've seen the panel, "The gate panel has no fuses".
- A stage shows as soon as the one before it is done, so check what the player has seen **at that
  moment**, not by the end of the stage. If it depends on the route, write the vaguer line.
- Guide entries already gate on flags (`cond`); give each the flag of the moment it becomes known.
- Check it the way a new player would: play to the stage, read it, and ask "how would I know that?"
