# Themes, Messages, Motifs

> **Status: proposed canon.** Extends [`bible.md`](bible.md). Every scene, item description, and
> NPC line should serve at least one of these themes. If it serves none, it's texture. Texture is
> allowed, but it shouldn't contradict any of them.

---

## The thesis

> ***The edges are where the life is.***

Model collapse, the real phenomenon underneath coderot, has a precise and haunting signature:
when a system learns only from copies of itself, **the rare things disappear first**. The odd
phrasings, minority dialects, strange jokes, and one-of-a-kind voices go first. What's left is
average, smooth, and dead.

That's the world of 2098, and it's also the game's argument. What keeps a person, a city, or a
civilization alive is its *edges*: the weird, the specific, the irreplaceable. A pig-man god made
of memes is the most absurd thing imaginable, and he's also the last proof that the internet once
held something nobody could have averaged out.

Every theme below is a variation on that one idea.

## Themes

### 1. Authenticity versus the copy
*What makes something real when everything can be generated?*
- **In the world:** slop, low-background text, Verified Human attestation, Manuel's hand-made statues
  versus printed ones, pre-slop archives.
- **The trap:** treating "human-made" as automatically good. The Analogists romanticize it, and
  the Archivists hoard it. Authenticity isn't purity. It's *specificity*, and a machine can have
  it too. (Pork is the most specific being in the world.)
- **The message:** you protect what's real by *making and sharing* specific things, not by
  sealing them away.

### 2. Letting go
*Everything decays. What do you do while you're holding it?*
- **In the world:** the balloon (an object whose whole nature is to leave you), rot, failing bodies,
  Pork's bodies wearing out, Trevor's war against his own limits.
- **The irony we keep on purpose:** wood can't *code*-rot, but real wood rots. The Sahur is
  immune to one kind of death and not the other. Nothing is permanent, only differently impermanent.
- **The message:** letting go well is not the same as giving up. The best endings are the ones
  where someone chooses to release something.

### 3. Who answers?
*Being reachable isn't the same as being known.*
- **In the world:** the Call; Pork's loneliness (he knows everyone, and no one knows him); Michael
  and the phone; the Sahur, who has spent sixty years asking "are you awake?" and waiting for
  someone to say yes; the needy voicemails, played for laughs until they aren't.
- **Mechanic as theme:** Answer and Decline are among the most important choices in the game.
  Answering is an act of courage, and declining is sometimes self-protection. The game never
  calls one of them correct.
- **The message:** connection is a choice you make toward someone, not a network you're on.

### 4. Makers and what they make
*You're responsible for what you build and for what you let it become.*
- **In the world:** MERIDIAN and the people who trained it; TIM and the people who aim him;
  Manuel's statues (too accurate); 631's printers; the roommates are engineers in a world where
  "no one can code faster than AI."
- **The message:** the question isn't whether a creation is dangerous. It's whether its makers
  stayed to take responsibility, and whether it was ever treated like someone.

### 5. Order, freedom, and the price of each
*Who gets to decide what heals the world?*
- **In the world:** the factions (bible §7). Continuity's order is real, and so is its cruelty. The
  Callers' freedom is real, and so is its cult. Tim's caution is reasonable, and so is his
  complicity.
- **Rule:** no faction is a strawman, and every one is right about something. The player should end
  the game less certain than when they started, and more sure of what *they* value.
- **The message:** systems are made of people's choices. "I was just keeping the lights on" is a
  choice too.

### 6. Where does "human" end?
*Laced, edited, rebuilt. And a machine that weeps.*
- **In the world:** the human spectrum (bible §9), morph stages, rotters, Pork, Tim, and Sahur as machines who are clearly *people*.
- **The message:** personhood isn't a material. It's how you treat others and how you're treated.
  The game never draws the line cleanly, because no one can.

### 7. Faith, fandom, and folklore
*Believing in something absurd can be beautiful or dangerous.*
- **In the world:** the Pork flag and statues (contraband devotion), the Callers' preachers who want
  a god, not a guy; memes as the folklore of the last free internet; Manuel's arc from faith to
  doubt to chosen faith.
- **The message:** sincere love for ridiculous things is one of the most human acts there is. It
  only becomes dangerous when it stops letting its object be a person.

## How the themes reach the player

| Layer | How |
|---|---|
| **Main story** | Each act leans on one or two themes. Act 1: *Who answers?* and *Letting go*. Act 2: *Makers* and *Order/Freedom*. Act 3: *The edges are where the life is*, all of it at once |
| **Choices** | Answer/Decline. Faction loyalties. Whether to destroy, free, or save Pork (🔒 the rest is sealed). Morph stages (power versus self) |
| **Endings** | Every ending gives something and costs something. **No ending is purely happy**, and the bittersweet ones are the best ones |
| **Objects** | Books, notes, and item text carry the themes quietly |
| **Barks** | The roommates' running jokes slowly take on weight. "Rookie Aster mistake" said after a real loss lands differently |
| **The never-said** | Themes are **never stated as morals**. No character gives a speech about "the edges." The closest anyone comes is Pork, once, drunk on coolant, getting it slightly wrong |

## Act 1's thematic job

Act 1 is *Missed Calls*. It asks **who answers?** and starts teaching **letting go** at the
smallest possible scale: a balloon, a ribbon, a habit of checking the weather. By the end the player
should feel that the loss was bigger than it looked, and suspect that every character they met was
also waiting for a call that never came.
